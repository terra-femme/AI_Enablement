# Lesson 3 — How to Ask (Prompting Basics)
**Time:** 8 min · **Core message:** *Better input, better output — give it context, a role, an example, and a format.*

## Objective
The room can turn a weak one-line prompt into a strong one using a simple 4-part recipe.

## Demo first (3 min) — the upgrade
1. Type a lazy prompt live: *"write an email to a client."* Show the bland result.
2. Upgrade it live with the recipe below. Show the dramatically better result.
The before/after is the whole lesson — let them *see* the difference.

## The recipe (rule of 4, but teach it as "CREF")
1. **C — Context:** who it's for and the situation. *"A client is unhappy about a delayed report."*
2. **R — Role:** who Claude should act as. *"You're a calm, professional account manager."*
3. **E — Example:** show a sample or our usual tone (optional but powerful).
4. **F — Format:** what you want back. *"3 short paragraphs, warm but accountable, no jargon."*

> If they remember nothing else: **"Tell it who it's helping, who to be, and what shape you want back."**

## The anchor analogy
> A prompt is a **delegation, not a search.** You'd never tell a new intern "write an email" with no context and expect gold. Brief it like you'd brief a person.

## Talking points / script beats
- "You don't need fancy 'prompt engineering.' You need to communicate like a good manager."
- "If the answer's off, don't start over — *tell it what to fix.* It's a conversation."

## Quick interactive (1 min)
Have someone give you a vague task; build the CREF prompt together out loud.

## Common stumble (and the fix)
- People accept the first answer. → Teach the follow-up: "make it shorter," "more formal," "add a deadline." Iteration is normal and expected.

## So what / now what
**Now what:** keep your best prompts. (Bridges to L5 — a team prompt library.)
